The War — Subtitle Translation Background

Series identity

The War is a seven-part American television documentary miniseries (type: 纪录片迷你剧) about the Second World War as experienced by the United States. Directed by Ken Burns (肯·伯恩斯) and Lynn Novick (琳恩·诺维克); written by Geoffrey C. Ward (杰弗里·C·沃德); narrated primarily by Keith David (基思·大卫). Original English. Country of origin: United States. Broadcaster: PBS (美国公共广播公司). Production: Florentine Films and WETA Washington, DC; funded in part by the National Endowment for the Humanities (国家人文基金会) and PBS, plus corporate underwriters General Motors, Anheuser-Busch and Bank of America. Composer: Wynton Marsalis (温顿·马萨利斯); venue song "American Anthem" performed by Norah Jones (诺拉·琼斯). First aired: September 23, 2007 (2007年9月23日); concluded: October 2, 2007 (2007年10月2日). Total runtime approximately 14–14.5 hours across two weeks (first week four consecutive nights, second week three nights). In some countries (Australia, Switzerland, Austria, France, Germany) the release was split into 14 half-hour episodes. Keith David won the 2008 Primetime Emmy Award for Outstanding Voice-Over Performance (艾美奖最佳配音). Festival de Cannes 2007 Official Selection, Special Session.

The acronym epigraph — The Second World War was fought in thousands of places, too many for any one accounting. This is the story of four American towns and how their citizens experienced that war. — appears before every episode.

World setting and overall premise

The documentary tells the story of the Second World War (第二次世界大战) "from the bottom up," through the personal accounts of more than forty men and women from four geographically distributed "quintessentially American towns" (四个具有代表性的美国小镇) that stand in for every town in the United States:

Luverne, Minnesota (明尼苏达州卢弗恩镇) — a small farming town in the Midwest; of the 21 male graduates of Luverne High School's class of 1939, 20 served in the armed forces. Anchored by pilot Quentin Aanenson and the writings of local newspaper editor Al McIntosh (read on-camera by Tom Hanks 汤姆·汉克斯).
Mobile, Alabama (亚拉巴马州莫比尔市) — a southern port city whose shipyards employed many African Americans; more than 15,000 people from Mobile served and 300 died in action. Anchored by Marines Sidney Phillips (西德尼·菲利普斯) and Eugene B. Sledge (尤金·B·斯莱奇), and Glenn Frazier.
Sacramento, California (加利福尼亚州萨克拉门托市) — a western boomtown ringed by three military bases. Anchored by Robert Kashiwagi, Harry Schmid, Sascha Weinzheimer, Burnett Miller, Walter Thompson, William Perkins, Tim Tokuno, Susumu Satow, Earl Burke.
Waterbury, Connecticut (康涅狄格州沃特伯里市) — the northeastern "Brass City" manufacturing hub. Anchored by Babe Ciarlo, Leo Goldberg, Ward Chamberlin, Joseph Vaghi.

Narrative spine: the war comes home to these towns with the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor (珍珠港) on December 7, 1941, and follows Americans through the Pacific, North African, Italian and European theaters, and on the home front, across roughly fifteen hours. The series also addresses the internment of Japanese Americans (日裔美国人拘禁), African American service under segregation, Native American (Native: American Indian 美洲原住民 /印第安人) service including the 442nd Regimental Combat Team (第442步兵团战斗队), the Women's Army Corps, the rescue of the "Lost Battalion" in the Battle of Hürtgen Forest, the liberation of the Santo Tomas Internment Camp in Manila, combat stress reaction ("battle fatigue" 战斗疲劳症), the strategic bombing campaign, the Battle of the Bulge, Iwo Jima, Okinawa, the death of Franklin D. Roosevelt and the accession of Harry Truman, the discovery of the Nazi concentration camps (纳粹集中营), the sinking of USS Indianapolis, the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and V-E and V-J Day (欧洲胜利日 / 对日战争胜利日). The series closes with the return and reintegration of the veterans and the long aftermath for the towns.

Speaking style and tonal notes for translation

- The narration by Keith David is slow, measured, and elegiac, in formal documentary register. Captions should be tight, written prose rather than conversational.
- Veteran interviews use plain, unadorned first-person speech with regional accents (Southern, Midwestern, New England). Preserve occasional hesitation, false starts, and the pause-before-recollection rhythm Burns uses. Where veterans narrate over archive, captions are typically short, single-sentence beats aligned to the edit.
- Voice-actor readings of journals, letters, and newspaper copy (Tom Hanks for Al McIntosh; Josh Lucas for Eugene Sledge) are read in stylized American English — Sledge's reading is deliberately Southern-accented; McIntosh's is small-town Midwestern. Mark these captions with the speaker / source line where one appears on screen.
- Period music (Benny Goodman, Artie Shaw, Glenn Miller "American Patrol") frequently underscores quiet domestic scenes — captions must not collide with song lyrics; translate song text only when on-screen text requires it.
- The series was provided to PBS affiliates in two versions: one with profanity (including explained use of FUBAR and SNAFU), one FCC-friendly without. Translate FUBAR (and SNAFU) to its explained English form with the Chinese gloss noted above; do not soften without flagging, since both versions exist.
- Title card "the story of four American towns and how their citizens experienced that war" recurs; reuse one canonical Chinese rendering rather than re-translating.

Misc translation guidance

- Names of real American towns and WWII historical places follow standard Chinese reference names listed above; do not transliterate afresh.
- For less-common witness surnames that have no established Chinese rendering in available sources, retain the English spelling with a parenthetical on first introduction, then use a consistent short Chinese phonetic gloss thereafter.
- Burns's signature visual devices — slow push-ins on stills, cutaways to letters with the envelope shown, recurring time markers ("December 1941," "July 1945") — are present throughout; translate all on-screen lower-third text faithfully, including credit blocks.
- The companion Library of Congress Veterans History Project tie-in is mentioned in PBS press materials; do not invent further material not on screen.
