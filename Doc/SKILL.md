# Background — Doc (Fox, 2025– )

Verification summary
- Title: Doc (American medical drama TV series)
- First air date: January 7, 2025
- Broadcaster: Fox Broadcasting Company (US); streaming on Hulu the day after broadcast
- Showrunners / developer: Barbie Kligman (developer), Hank Steinberg (co-showrunner)
- Production: Sony Pictures Television, Fox Entertainment Studios, Channel Road Productions, Avenue K Productions
- Format: 3 seasons, 34 episodes total — S1 10 ep (2025-01-07 to 2025-03-18), S2 22 ep (2025-09-14 to 2026-04-14), S3 22 ep (from 2026-09-22, airing)
- Genre: Medical drama; single-protagonist case-of-the-week serialized structure
- Based on: Italian series Doc – Nelle tue mani (Rai 1, 2020–), itself inspired by the true story of Dr. Pierdante Piccioni
- Filmed in Toronto; set in Minneapolis

Series premise
Dr. Amy Larsen, chief of internal medicine at the fictional Westside Hospital in Minneapolis, suffers a traumatic brain injury in a car accident that erases the previous eight years of memory. She reawakens having already been divorced, distanced from her daughter, and isolated from colleagues who once feared her. Stripped of her chief title, she must rebuild her medical career, rediscover who she became, and decide whether the colder, harder woman she used to be is who she wants to remain. Each episode balances a self-contained medical case with the slow excavation of her lost years and the relationships she is forced to relearn.

World setting
Fictional Westside Hospital, an inner-city teaching hospital in Minneapolis, Minnesota, affiliated with an internal-medicine residency program and a cardiology fellowship. The hospital's hierarchy is run by a chairman of the board (Max Garner) and a chief medical officer (later chief of internal medicine); below them are attending physicians, fellows, chief residents, senior residents, interns, and nursing staff. Sister facility "Eastside Hospital" exists within the same network. Episodes are framed around rounds, consultations, the ED, ICU, and outpatient clinic; medical ethics, resource allocation, and family consent are recurring through-lines. The series does not depict seasons/weather shifts consistently; Minneapolis geography is mentioned but rarely shown.

Key relationships
- Amy and Michael were married and divorced in the erased eight years; they co-parent Katie and must work together as senior staff.
- Amy and Jake had an affair/relationship that ended before the accident; Jake still cares for her in S1–S2.
- Amy and Gina share a long friendship; Gina is Amy's main confidante and treating neuropsychiatrist.
- Sonya and TJ are the central younger-couple pairing from S1 onward.
- Joan Ridley is Amy's professional mentor; her death (between S2 and S3) reshapes the department's leadership.
- Richard Miller and Amy were rivals for the chief role; he briefly held the post.
- Katie Hamda is caught between her parents and resents the mother she only now meets as a stranger.

Speaking styles and tone
Dialogue is contemporary American clinical English, marked by fast-paced medical jargon delivered in clipped, urgent register during emergencies and a slower, emotionally expository register in one-on-one scenes. Amy speaks in two distinct voices: pre-accident (referenced in flashbacks and in others' recollections) — clipped, authoritative, occasionally caustic; post-accident — softer, reflective, often confused, frequently self-correcting. Gina offers wry, supportive clinical commentary; Michael is measured and diplomatic; Jake uses earnest, sometimes pleading tone with patients; Sonya is direct, scientifically rigorous; TJ is informal and uses humor to manage stress. Patients and family members span regional American accents; medical voice-overs and case-presentation language (e.g., "presents with…", "history of present illness", "differential includes…") recur.

Cultural context
- US residency system and teaching hospital hierarchy are central to character dynamics; viewers unfamiliar with the attending/fellow/resident/intern ladder should have those roles clarified in early lines.
- Informed consent, end-of-life decision-making, medical futility, and patient autonomy are recurring ethical backdrops, reflecting standard US clinical practice.
- Minneapolis setting is referenced rather than shown; the cityscape is not integral.
- The series inherits the Italian original's central premise of a physician forced to confront who they became, but resets the location, characters, and inciting event to a US hospital and a car accident (the Italian original used a gunshot).
- The original "Doc – Nelle tue mani" is often cited in press materials; subtitles that reference the Italian version should translate back to its on-screen US analogue and not import Andrea Fanti or the Milan Policlinico Ambrosiano terminology.

Translation pitfalls to avoid
- Do not confuse this series with the 1975 CBS sitcom Doc, the 2001 Pax TV medical drama of the same name, or the Italian source series Doc – Nelle tue mani.
- Do not import the Italian source's character names (Andrea Fanti, Giulia Giordano, Policlinico Ambrosiano) when translating the US adaptation; only refer to the Italian original in meta or flashback framing.
- "Doc" in dialogue is the residents' nickname for Amy; render consistently (建议：保留 "Doc" 不译，或统一译作"大夫/医生")。
- "Internal medicine" must remain 内科; do not conflate with 内科医学 (academic field) or 普通内科 colloquialisms.
- Medical jargon should retain English abbreviations (ICU, DNR, TBI) on first mention with Chinese gloss, then use either consistently thereafter.
