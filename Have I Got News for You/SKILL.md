# Have I Got News for You — Subtitle Translation Background

## Series identification

- English title: Have I Got News for You (abbreviated HIGNFY)
- Established Chinese title (Taiwan/traditional): 新聞問答
- Established Chinese title (mainland, partial renderings seen): 《拍案新闻》 (literal/colloquial mainland academic reference); BBC中文网 typically leaves the English title in roman letters when referencing it.
- Format: British comedy panel game / political satire (喜劇類電視節目 / 政治諷刺益智節目)
- First aired: 28 September 1990
- Broadcaster: BBC Two (1990 – 2 June 2000), then BBC One (20 October 2000 – present); produced by Hat Trick Productions
- Created by: Jimmy Mulville (Hat Trick); concept derived from BBC Radio 4's The News Quiz
- Total output (as of mid-2026): 72 series, ~640+ episodes; running per series is split into a Spring series (April–June) and an Autumn series (October–December), with the Autumn run taking one week off for the Children in Need special
- Episode length: ~30 minutes standard, ~42 minutes for the extended cut ("Have I Got a Bit More News for You", originally aired the day after the standard episode)
- Production locations: The London Studios (1990–2017), Elstree Studios (2018–2019), Riverside Studios (2020–present)
- This is NOT the 1950s American series of the same title; confirm episode metadata shows a 1990 or later UK airdate before translating.

## Relationships & on-screen roles

- Two regular team captains: Ian Hislop and Paul Merton have appeared in essentially every episode since the pilot. They sit opposite each other on the panel.
- Each captain is paired with one guest panellist per episode, forming two teams of two.
- Host / chair (主持人) presides over the quiz, reads out news questions, and adjudicates. Angus Deayton held this role 1990–2002; since 2002 it has been a different guest presenter each week.
- "Team captain" is a role, not a leadership position — it is closer to "固定班底隊長" in Chinese. Translators should avoid rendering it as "隊長" alone in subtitle context; a gloss like "固定班底 / 隊長" is acceptable.
- The relationship between Hislop (journalist, satirist) and Merton (improviser, surrealist comedian) is the show's core dynamic: dry, deadpan political snark vs. absurdist improvisation. Subtitle lines frequently rely on this contrast — Merton's non-sequiturs should be rendered with the same absurd tonal register, not "corrected" for logic.
- Deayton's departure (2002) is a recurring in-joke: guests and team captains routinely mock whichever presenter is currently chairing, and Deayton himself was the butt of jokes about drugs, prostitution and tabloid scandal for the rest of his tenure.

## World setting

- Real-world contemporary Britain. The programme has no fictional setting, no recurring fictional characters; it comments on actual news of the past week.
- Recorded the day before broadcast in a BBC studio (London since 2020 at Riverside Studios), with topical material finalised only hours before recording so guests can react to the latest headlines.
- Episodes are edited from a longer recording (typically ~60 minutes filmed) down to the broadcast length partly so BBC lawyers can defuse potential libel claims.
- The show exists in a parallel universe of British political life: Whitehall, Westminster, Downing Street, the Palace, the City, tabloid press, BBC News, and recurring public figures (politicians, royals, broadcasters) are the substrate of every round.
- Cultural shorthand the show trades on: Private Eye (the magazine Hislop edits), The News Quiz (the Radio 4 parent show), Spitting Image (1990s puppet satire), Spitting Image-style public-figure mockery, parliamentary jargon, Fleet Street / tabloid vocabulary.

## Overall plot premise

There is no serialized narrative. Each episode is self-contained and structured roughly as:

1. Opening monologue by the guest presenter, typically lampooning the news of the week with visual gags (costumes, props, print-outs of tabloid front pages — most famously, Merton's "front page on a T-shirt" jibe at Deayton in 2002).
2. Rounds in which the two teams answer questions about the week's news. Common recurring rounds:
   - The Odd One Out Round (四幅圖中找出異類): panellists pick the picture that does not belong and explain the news behind it.
   - Missing Words Round (缺失詞彙填空): incomplete newspaper headlines are completed with invented words; panellists' funniest answers win regardless of factual accuracy.
   - Video / picture rounds where the host introduces clips and stills from the news, and teams are scored on improvised wisecracks rather than literal answers.
3. Guest presenters contribute their own running jokes (Boris Johnson's self-deprecation, Jeremy Clarkson's laddishness, etc.).
4. The extended cut ("Have I Got a Bit More News for You") adds unedited material from the same recording session, originally broadcast on BBC Two the following Saturday and later on BBC One on the following Monday.
5. Yearly compilation specials exist, e.g., "Have I Got 2024 News for You", plus a 30-year retrospective documentary "Have I Got 30 Years for You".

The "plot" of any episode is therefore: a guest presenter chairs two teams (Hislop + guest vs. Merton + guest) through a topical quiz whose actual scoring matters less than the satirical banter it produces.

## Speaking styles

- Ian Hislop: dry, lawyerly, clipped, fact-based; leans on Private Eye-style political mockery. Mandarin equivalent register: 冷面吐槽, 條理清晰, 嘲諷時政.
- Paul Merton: absurdist, surreal, improvisational, non-sequitur-heavy; often delivers long, tangential comedic riffs with a completely straight face. Mandarin equivalent: 一本正經地講荒唐話, 即興胡謅, 冷面滑稽.
- Angus Deayton (1990–2002): pseudo-authoritative, John Cleese-inflected, ironic; reads news with arched eyebrows. Mandarin equivalent: 假正經, 冷嘲式播報.
- Guest presenters vary widely: Boris Johnson is self-deprecatingly bumbling; Jeremy Clarkson is laddish and blunt; Bruce Forsyth is avuncular showbiz; Alexander Armstrong is smooth and actorly; Anne Robinson is acerbic. Subtitle tone must mirror the speaker's own register — do not normalise every line into the same neutral Mandarin.
- Overall programme register: 知識分子式英式幽默 / 紳士式吐槽, with frequent deadpan, irony, sarcasm, innuendo, and self-aware references to BBC institutional conventions.

## Cultural context for translators

- British understatement and deadpan humour are core to the show. Mandarin renderings should preserve the understated delivery rather than inflating jokes; "quite good, I suppose" should not become "非常出色". (See rules: avoid 是/不是 句式, avoid 一句话总结.)
- Political in-jokes assume viewer familiarity with Westminster, Whitehall, Downing Street, the Palace, the BBC, and tabloid press. When a line is incomprehensible without this context, add a brief gloss in parentheses or an unobtrusive subtitle line, but do not lecture.
- Historical figures (UK prime ministers, royals, party leaders, BBC presenters, tabloid editors) appear in almost every episode. Use the most widely-circulated Mandarin name (e.g., Theresa May → 特里莎·梅 / 梅首相, Boris Johnson → 鲍里斯·约翰逊, Queen Elizabeth II → 伊丽莎白二世女王, Donald Trump → 唐纳德·特朗普).
- "News of the World" was a real Sunday tabloid closed in 2011 over the phone-hacking scandal; the show referenced it heavily during the Deayton era (2002).
- Libel / "BBC lawyers" is a recurring self-aware joke: the show is edited to remove anything legally actionable, and panellists sometimes joke mid-sentence that a phrase will be cut.
- "Children in Need" (BBC charity telethon) and "Comic Relief" are the two BBC charity events the show regularly dovetails with; the Autumn series always pauses one week for Children in Need and a special episode typically airs during Comic Relief.
- The show is the longest-running panel quiz on British TV (since 1990) and a touchstone for British political satire; subtitles should treat it as canonical British comedy reference, not as an obscure programme.
- VoiceTube and other learning sites render the show title in Chinese as 《Have I Got News for You》 (English in title-case) with descriptive subtitles such as 「喜劇演員酸川普求助伊朗！」, indicating that Chinese-speaking audiences most often encounter the show under its English title with a Chinese descriptive gloss.

## Notes on name/term conflict resolution

Historical ASSRT references below ID 800000 returned no matches for this title. Where Chinese renderings conflict across sources, the order of precedence used here is:

1. Chinese Wikipedia (zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/新聞問答) for the show's overall Chinese title (新聞問答 in traditional).
2. Mainland academic / journalistic references (盘古书库 《媒介融合时代的电视新闻创新》 chapter26) for character names (保罗·莫顿, 伊恩·西斯罗普, 安格斯·迪顿) and the descriptive title 《拍案新闻》. Note the academic source uses "西斯罗普" for Hislop; the broader BBC-mandarin usage (and this translator's preferred default) is "希斯洛普". Either is defensible.
3. BBC中文网 (bbc.com/ukchina) for BBC-house Chinese renderings of recurring guest names (杰里米·克拉克森).
4. VoiceTube and other learning-media descriptive titles, used only as evidence of how Chinese-speaking audiences actually encounter the show, not as authoritative terminology.
