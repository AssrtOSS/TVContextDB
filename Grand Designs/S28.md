## S28E01

## Grand Designs S28E01 Summary

### Episode Plot
Matt (CFO of a London renewable energy firm) and Bryony (former teacher, worked in Papua New Guinea and Borneo) build a high-performance eco-home in North Cornwall after moving from Manchester in 2019. With children Atlas (born 6 weeks early) and Seren (2, developmental delay — "a bundle of determined joy"), they aim for Passivhaus standards on a £820k budget (£1M cap). The stick-built timber frame uses recycled plastic slab reinforcement, 380mm total insulation, triple glazing, recycled newspaper/wood-fibre insulation, MVHR ventilation, and zero traditional heating. Architect Mark Inness (met at his earlier inspirational build) designs 242 unique hand-cut trusses. Construction takes 18 months (6 over schedule); final cost ~£970–980k. A neighbour’s approved extension (ridge +1.2m) partially blocks the sea view. Air-tightness test barely passes Passivhaus. They name the finished house **Cloud House**.

### Important Character Actions
- **Matt**: Weekly 6-hour London commute; works from home 2 days/week; helps on site ("just the muscle"); tracks finances as CFO; admits budget "egg on face."
- **Bryony**: Takes full interior reins ("complete free rein") — disco bathroom, 9–10 handle styles, octopus wallpaper, funky mirrors, secret yoga room, outdoor shower; organises site Christmas party to ease tension; manages overwhelm with clipboard "brain" system.
- **Together**: Maintain trust with Mark despite evolving drawings; "still speaking, still playing poker together" after 18 months.

## S28E02

# Grand Designs S28E02 Summary

## Episode Plot
Architect **Peter** and TV producer **Emily** build the "Cloud House" — a pair of conjoined, postmodern pink houses in Kentish Town, north London. The project comprises two semi-detached homes on adjacent plots (their old house + former garden), funded partly by pre-selling the neighbouring house to **Thuza** (business consultant/supper club host) and **Philippe** (law lecturer). The design features 11 circular windows, 30 arched windows, a repeating 1.2 m arch rhythm, Miami Art Deco–inspired pink render, turquoise frames, and polystyrene decorative arches. Construction spans ~3 years, plagued by budget shortfalls, a contractor change (Bart → Chris), a 9-month hiatus, and precision alignment nightmares. Both houses complete; Peter and Emily move in 99% finished at £735k (5% over £700k budget); Thuza and Philippe finish later, under £850k, with a newborn daughter Clementine.

## Important Character Actions
- **Peter**: Designs the Cloud House as his "masterpiece" — a radical departure from his usual hushed-elegance refurbishments. Obsesses over millimetre-perfect arch alignment; personally installs 300 polystyrene arches with daughter Astor; paints entire interior himself to save money. Shifts from perfectionist to realist when arches misalign by 30–50 mm.
- **Emily**: Project-manages; loses TV-industry work during build (6+ months unemployed), creating £120k funding gap. Secures self-build mortgage (£70k short); parents eventually lend completion funds. Moves in with Peter and daughter Astor 10 days before final visit.
- **Thuza & Philippe**: Buy neighbouring plot off-plan via standard property site; bond instantly with Peter/Emily. Thuza inherits funds after father’s Covid death ("rather have a father than the house"). Pregnant during build; baby Clementine born before completion. Philippe becomes DIY expert to contain costs.
- **Bart (original builder)**: 6 weeks behind on 15-month schedule; disputes with Peter over 30 mm width discrepancy and window positions. Peter terminates contract; Bart leaves site.
- **Chris (replacement builder)**: Takes over at same price as Bart; completes structure and render in +3 months. Praised as "knight in shining armour."

## Newly Established Names / Terms
- **Cloud House** — project name for the conjoined pink pair
- **Pink Palace** — Kevin’s nickname for the design
- **Postmodern arches** — 1.2 m grid, offset per floor, purely decorative (not structural)
- **Polystyrene arches** — high-density EPS, CNC hot-wire cut, £62k for 300 pieces ("super puzzle")
- **Strawberry fondant render** — final pink textured coating
- **Cosmic House** — Charles & Maggie Jencks’ Holland Park postmodern interior (1983), Peter’s key inspiration
- **Chromophobia** — David Batchelor’s book on Western fear of colour, referenced in Kevin’s closing monologue

## Continuity-Relevant Facts
- Peter’s first ground-up new build (previous work: refurbishments/extensions)
- Two-house collaborative funding model: one sale finances both builds
- Self-build mortgage difficulty for freelancers (Peter & Emily both freelance)
- Planning took 4 years despite eclectic local architecture
- Construction method switched from timber frame → traditional blockwork due to timber price spike
- 41 custom windows (turquoise frames, circular/arched) cost £167k total
- Final render rescue package: £40k to correct arch misalignments

## S28E05

# Episode Plot

A revisit episode. Kevin returns to Netherton, a village near Huddersfield in West Yorkshire, to check on Chris and Gill, whom he first filmed in 1999 buying a derelict 200-year-old textile mill sight unseen ("What a dump!"). The original story is replayed first: the couple stripped the shell out, rebuilt the unsafe road-facing gable and roof with the original stone and slates, and inserted a modernist interior — an exposed "noughts and crosses" steel frame on four big pins carrying the first floor and lightwells, a full-height central atrium, glazed roof openings, a curved wall shower visible from almost everywhere, and a six-month, millimetre-perfect steel staircase. Work ran February to October: ten months, on schedule and on budget, at £50,000 for mill and land plus a £150,000 build budget (£200,000 total, all from savings).

The main build drama was structural: the new long steel beams fouled the old timber beams holding the building up, so they had to remove them and hope the 200-year-old walls wouldn't spread. They didn't collapse, but the rough steelwork ("like a badger's backside") sent the fabricators back to grind and sand welds.

25 years on, Kevin finds the couple and the house essentially unchanged — now with a Mediterranean garden of palms, sculptures and reclaimed flags, and an interior Kevin calls better than it was. The house, he concludes, has almost earned heritage status: nothing was compromised, and it was built "fit for time."

# Important Character Actions

- **Chris and Gill**: bought the mill on a whim without going inside; ran the whole build themselves from "command HQ" — their joinery workshop 10 minutes away — with a master file, an "attention to detail file" and room-by-room boxes of fittings, each double-checked. Refused to hire a project manager. Sold their old cottage mid-build and moved, with their two dogs, into Gill's mother's house for eight weeks.
- They later lived in **Menorca for seven years** (burnt out; Chris wanted to go sailing), then returned.
- At the revisit: raised the boundary walls via Gill's brother's dry-stone walling for privacy; replaced the original black "spaceship" kitchen island with a timber one made from a tree they bought and machined themselves; kept the original music/hi-fi cabinet and lighting frame while upgrading internals (LEDs, speakers plastered into walls); added a small gym.
- **Sold the joinery business to the employees**; now landlords hoping to retire "in the not-too-distant future."
- Admitted putting the house up for sale about four months earlier after spotting another project; withdrew it when that fell through. Both call themselves "very impulsive."
- **Kevin**: re-evaluates the build, admitting it is better than he remembered and that his earlier doubts were about the style being too new ("it felt like a showroom... I was tiptoeing around").
